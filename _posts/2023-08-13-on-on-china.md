I read "On China" over a rather long period of time, frequently interrupted by work and other commitments and in some sense I feel I benefitted all the more from it. Each section of the book has its own flavor and I was lucky enough to savor them all without mixing them up. It also has a unique personal tone to it as the author is a diplomat who played a significant role in formulating the Sino-US relations.

The book starts off as a reminder in Chinese history, which is very significant because unlike with many victims of colonization, like the Australians, Canadians or the South Americans, the Chinese colonial experience did not involve a near complete destruction of the existing culture of the land. The Chinese fiercely hold on to their own culture and identity to this day. Chinese perceptions of strategy, international relations and political ideology in general are complex and guided by an amorphous way of thinking that sees all these aspects as part of the whole. The book repeatedly focuses on the concept of Shi, roughly translated to momentum which Sun Szu's famed Art of War preaches. The concept, to some extent, guides Chinese relations to this day, claims the author.

The book goes on to tell the tale of the emperors of China who had the 'heavenly mandate' to rule the world. Any other nation besides the great Chinese empire were barbarians who would be looked at with a benevolent generosity if they came in humility. This perception is crushed, rather cruelly, by colonial powers and following Japanese expansionists. The author seems slightly condescending as he narrates these portions.

The following sections of the book appeared , to me , the most interesting as this is the time period where the author was actively a diplomat . They deal with the personal portraits of the leaders, from the ideologue Mao, to the realist Deng to the amiable Jiang. Each get their chapters and vivid portrayals as the story of China is painted foremost and along with it, a growing picture of troubled Sino-US relations. The book closes with the author's views on what a possible future might look like.

I really enjoyed reading the intricacies of diplomacy that were laid out in the book. It emphasized the work done by diplomats in bridging the wide gaps in even cultural perceptions. The two countries were as far as they could be in every terms, be it physical distance, type of government or guiding ideology. But prevailing events, the threat of the USSR mainly, forced them to be unlikely allies and this book is a great guide on how that came to be. The world building in this book is all real and it is exquisite. Each Chinese leader and his US counterparts, their own political stances and the events of the world all ebb and flow the relationship among some of the most important governments in the world, and this book is a great testament to it.
