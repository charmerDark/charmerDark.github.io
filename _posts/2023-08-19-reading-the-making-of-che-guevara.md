There is something cathartic about reading the autobiography of the most revered revolutionary communist while nestled snugly in an MNC's office; maybe the class traitor in me finds it amusing. Nevertheless, "Motor Cycle Diaries" by Che Guevera was a most fascinating read. It deals not with his revolutionary times or philosophy but is an account of a trip he made with good friend Alberto Grando. It is an irreverent account of two tail chasing, wine and women loving young men going to see the continent they live in. They make grand plans of traversing across Latin America on *Pedrosa II,* Alberto's bike, one last romp before they settle down as a doctor and a biochemist, starting their professional life. If only they knew how wrong they were about their future.

<img src="/images/making_of_che_1.jpg" alt="The Making of Che Guevara" style="width: 100%;">

The book starts with a very light tone, detailing their start of journey and how they struggled with *Pedrosa II* as the automobile invariably broke some part or the other, how they met new villages and people, acquired fame in a newspaper as expert doctors and chronicling the many fun people on the way. But as time passes you can see the revolutionary grow in Che. He sees first hand the suffering of the latin american poor who sell their entire day to American owned mines in trade for barely enough money to work again the next day. He sees the communist couple who are forced into helplessness just for the crime of adhering to an ideology and who's only option now is the most dangerous mine where even ideology doesn't matter as long as they can pack the mine with bodies that will be consumed soon. Che sympathizes with the native people of the Americas through out the book, the once proud race that hoisted empires but struggle to fight off poverty after ages of colonization. The experiences transform Che Guevera and you can see the sound of bitterness in his tone from there on.

Another fascinating quality of the book is how the effortless tone and romanticisation of everyday life. The trip was grueling, especially on a severely asthmatic person such as Che and they made it through most of the journey asking for help and even hatching mischievous plots to convince the local doctors or other officials. They often had to sleep in the cold mountains, lay down in the hot deserts, were treated with disdain as hobos and went through a number of unpleasant circumstances, but that did not stop them from enjoying good *mate,* the argentine national drink, alcohol, great company and the various wonderful cities, rivers, oceans, deserts and mountains that they saw on the way. The book finds time to write about each and every one of them, admiring their history and beauty or bemoaning how times have not been kind to them in the least. Every day was a new adventure, not always the best, but an adventure anyway.

<img src="/images/making_of_che_2.jpg" alt="The Making of Che Guevara" style="width: 50%; display: block; margin: 0 auto;">

You could easily read the book as a travelogue of a cocky youth and leave it be if you didn't know that after the incidents of the book, the author went on to change the political landscape of the world forever in an effort to help the suffering he saw across his land. How many of us can claim something similar? We take a moment to bask in pity at the unfortunate very often in our lives, but Ernesto went on to take action to stave off the injustices he saw. Perhaps, being the face of resistance to injustice across the planet is the reward for his efforts.
